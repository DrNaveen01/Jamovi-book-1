10  Comparing Two Groups or Two Paired Measurements

10.1 Learning objectives

After this chapter, the reader should be able to distinguish independent from paired comparisons; define the contrast direction; choose between a mean-based and rank-based question; check the relevant assumptions; and report an estimate without reducing the analysis to a p-value.

10.2 Establish the comparison structure

Two groups are independent when each observation contributes to one group and the sampling or design does not create the paired dependence addressed by a paired method. Two measurements are paired when they belong to the same participant or to a defined matched pair. The distinction comes from the design, not from whether the group sample sizes happen to be equal.

Examples are mean follow-up blood pressure in separate groups A and B, and baseline versus follow-up blood pressure in the same participants. The first asks about a between-group contrast. The second asks about the mean within-participant change. These questions should not be analysed interchangeably.

Feature Independent comparison Paired comparison
Basic unit One observation in one group One complete pair
Main mean contrast Mean in A minus mean in B Mean of defined within-pair differences
Dependence handled Groups are independent under the simple model Matching is retained within pairs
Key graphical checks Group distributions and unusual observations Distribution of within-pair differences
Missingness Available outcome values in each group Availability of both measurements for the paired calculation

10.3 Decide what should be compared

A comparison of means targets an arithmetic difference. A rank-based comparison asks about the ordering or distribution of observations under its assumptions. A non-parametric test is not simply a mean test with the normality requirement removed.

Specify which quantity matters. If the objective concerns average resource use, a mean may remain relevant even when the distribution is skewed. If it concerns an ordered outcome or a distributional shift, a rank-based approach may be suitable. The choice should retain the original research objective.

10.4 Independent-samples t-tests

10.4.1 Welch’s method

Welch’s t-test estimates a mean difference using the two sample variances separately. It does not impose the equal-variance assumption of the pooled Student method. R documents the Welch/Satterthwaite degrees-of-freedom calculation and distinguishes it from the pooled-variance option. [38]

For groups A and B,

\widehat\Delta=\bar x_A-\bar x_B, \qquad SE(\widehat\Delta)=\sqrt{\frac{s_A^2}{n_A}+\frac{s_B^2}{n_B}}.

The test compares this difference with the specified null value using the appropriate t reference distribution. A confidence interval adds and subtracts the relevant critical value multiplied by the SE.

Welch’s method addresses unequal variances; it does not repair dependence, invalid coding, or an outcome dominated by a few influential observations. Inspect those issues separately. In small samples, major departures from the intended distributional model require particular attention.

10.4.2 Pooled Student’s method

The pooled independent t-test uses a shared variance estimate. Its interpretation requires a defensible equal-variance model in addition to the independent-observation and distributional conditions. A preliminary variance test alone should not determine whether those assumptions are scientifically reasonable.

If the pooled method is used, explain why the common-variance model is appropriate. If Welch’s method is used, report it by name rather than labelling every independent comparison simply as “a t-test.” The choice affects the SE and degrees of freedom when group variances or sizes differ.

10.4.3 Check the data before reading the test

Confirm the group labels, outcome unit, group counts, missingness, and contrast direction. Inspect each group’s distribution and check verified extreme values for their influence. Distinguish a coding error from a genuine unusual observation. The analysis should follow the specified estimand rather than change because one version gives a smaller p-value.

10.5 Calculated independent-group illustration

The following summaries are invented for teaching and are not extracted from a patient dataset:

Group n Mean systolic blood pressure, mmHg SD, mmHg
A 60 128.0 14.0
B 60 134.0 16.0

The contrast A minus B is −6.0 mmHg. Its Welch SE is 2.745 mmHg. The t statistic is −2.186, with approximately 115.96 degrees of freedom. The two-sided p-value is 0.0308 and the 95% CI is −11.44 to −0.56 mmHg. These quantities are reproduced in the project’s verification script.

The estimate indicates a lower mean in A. The interval excludes zero but still allows a wide range of reductions. If 5 mmHg is used as an explicitly illustrative importance threshold, the interval does not establish that the reduction is at least that large. The study design, rather than the t-test alone, determines whether the difference can be interpreted causally.

10.5.1 Reading the output

Locate the row for the chosen procedure and confirm the sign of the mean difference. Read the effect estimate and CI before the p-value. The degrees of freedom may be non-integer for Welch’s procedure; this is a property of its approximation, not a sign that participants were counted fractionally.

jamovi’s independent-samples analysis assigns a continuous outcome and a two-level grouping variable. The author will add the dataset, options, and output screenshots in the Hands-on section. [39]

10.5.2 A reasoning record for the illustration

The summary calculations can be reviewed now, while the practical demonstration remains reserved for the author. A complete reasoning record connects the numerical result with its intended question and its limits:

Stage Decision or finding
Question Compare mean follow-up systolic blood pressure in groups A and B
Target The unadjusted mean difference, A minus B, in mmHg
Structure One participant contributes to one group; this independence is an illustration assumption
Preparation Verify group labels, outcome unit, eligibility, missingness, and one observation per participant
Procedure Welch’s two-sided comparison of means, with a 95% CI
Numerical finding Estimated difference -6.0 mmHg; interval -11.44 to -0.56 mmHg
Meaning A lower estimated mean in A, with uncertainty about the magnitude
Reporting Include group summaries, contrast direction, CI, method, and design limitations

The two summary rows are sufficient for the stated Welch calculation. They are insufficient to inspect distributions, detect influential values, establish the missingness pattern, verify the observation unit, or establish a causal interpretation. Those checks require the study record and individual observations. Do not label them as completed merely because the calculation can be reproduced.

If the scientific objective is a baseline-adjusted group contrast, this unadjusted result does not answer the full question. If observations are clustered or repeated, the assumed independent structure is inadequate. These are reasons to revise the analytical method while preserving the stated objective, rather than simply choose another test from the same menu.

Reversing the contrast to B minus A gives +6.0 mmHg and a 95% CI of +0.56 to +11.44 mmHg. The two-sided evidence is unchanged, but the reporting direction must change with it. This is a useful consistency check when reviewing the future jamovi output.

10.6 Paired t-test

The paired t-test analyses the within-pair differences. Define the direction, such as follow-up minus baseline, calculate a difference for each complete pair, and estimate the mean difference with its SE. The distributional assumption used for the conventional small-sample t inference concerns the differences, not separate normality tests for the two measurement columns. [40]

For differences d_i,

\bar d=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_i, \qquad SE(\bar d)=\frac{s_d}{\sqrt n}.

The model requires independence between the pairs for this simple analysis. It does not require independence between the two measurements within each pair; that dependence is why the pairing is retained.

Confirm matching by identifier rather than row order alone when measurements were imported from separate files. Excluding a participant with one missing measurement changes the complete-pair sample. Report how many pairs are available and examine whether incomplete follow-up limits the interpretation.

10.6.1 Calculated paired illustration

In an invented example of 30 complete pairs, the mean baseline value is 146 mmHg and the mean follow-up value is 138 mmHg. The mean difference, follow-up minus baseline, is −8.0 mmHg, and the SD of the differences is 10.0 mmHg.

The paired SE is 10/\sqrt{30}=1.826 mmHg. The t statistic is −4.382 with 29 degrees of freedom. The 95% CI for mean change is −11.73 to −4.27 mmHg, and the two-sided p-value is 0.000141. Report p < 0.001 at an appropriate precision.

The estimate describes average within-participant change. A before–after study without an appropriate comparison does not isolate the reason for that change. Time trends, additional care, regression to the mean, and incomplete follow-up may remain relevant. The statistical result should not be labelled a treatment effect solely because the change is non-zero.

jamovi’s paired analysis requires the defined measurement pairs and can provide mean-difference, interval, descriptive, and diagnostic outputs. Check the sign convention used in its displayed comparison against the convention specified in the manuscript. [41]

10.7 Mann–Whitney U test

The Mann–Whitney test uses the relative ranks of observations in two independent groups. It is useful for an ordered or distributional comparison under its assumptions. An interpretation as a simple location or median shift requires additional conditions, such as comparable distributional shapes; it is not automatically a test of two medians. [42]

For a small invented illustration, let A contain 2, 4, 6, and 8, and B contain 1, 3, 5, and 7. The combined ranks for A are 2, 4, 6, and 8, with rank sum 20. Subtracting 4(4+1)/2=10 gives one convention for U_A=10.

Among the 16 A–B pairs, A is higher in 10, so the observed probability-of-superiority summary is 10/16 = 0.625, with no ties. This calculation helps explain the ordering information. It does not replace a test or interval appropriate to the chosen estimand, and the sample is too small for a precise population conclusion.

Software may report a rank-sum statistic or a transformed U statistic. Identify the definition when reproducing output. Ties and exact versus asymptotic calculations can also affect the reported p-value, so record the selected approach.

10.8 Wilcoxon signed-rank test

The signed-rank test is for paired differences or a one-sample difference distribution. It ranks the non-zero absolute differences and uses their signs. Its conventional location interpretation assumes a suitably symmetric difference distribution; it is not an assumption-free alternative for any paired dataset. [42]

For invented differences −4, −2, −1, 3, and 6, the ranks of the absolute differences are 4, 2, 1, 3, and 5. The positive rank sum is 3 + 5 = 8, and the negative rank sum is 4 + 2 + 1 = 7. This example shows why the signed-rank method uses both direction and ranked magnitude, rather than just counting positive changes.

If a difference distribution is strongly asymmetric, consider whether a different estimand or method is required. The paired t-test targets a mean change; the signed-rank procedure targets a different distributional feature. Changing tests should not be described as preserving the same question automatically.

10.9 Effect size and reporting

Retain the effect on its original scale when that scale is clinically interpretable. A −6-mmHg difference communicates more directly than a standardised label alone. If a standardised effect is added, define its denominator and sign convention. Paired and independent standardisations are not interchangeable.

A reporting sentence for the independent illustration is: “Group A had a lower mean follow-up systolic blood pressure than group B (128.0 versus 134.0 mmHg; n = 60 in each group). The mean difference, A minus B, was −6.0 mmHg (95% CI −11.4 to −0.6; Welch t = −2.19, df = 115.96, p = 0.031).”

For the paired illustration: “Among 30 complete pairs, mean follow-up minus baseline systolic blood pressure was −8.0 mmHg (SD of differences 10.0; 95% CI −11.7 to −4.3; paired t = −4.38, df = 29, p < 0.001).” The interpretation should then state the design limitations relevant to attributing the change.

10.11 Review questions

  1. Two groups have the same number of observations. Does this make them paired?
  2. Which distribution is relevant to the paired t-test’s conventional normality assumption?
  3. What does Welch’s procedure address, and what does it leave unresolved?
  4. Why is a Mann–Whitney result not automatically evidence of different medians?

10.12 Answers and reasoning

  1. No. Pairing requires a defined relationship between observations.
  2. The within-pair differences.
  3. It relaxes the pooled equal-variance assumption. It does not resolve dependence, biased sampling, invalid coding, or every form of influential non-normality.
  4. The procedure uses ranks and distributional ordering. A pure median or location-shift interpretation needs additional distributional conditions.
