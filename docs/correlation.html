13.2 Define the association question

Consider whether age and systolic blood pressure vary together among study participants. Each participant contributes one age–blood-pressure pair. Neither variable is automatically an outcome in the correlation calculation; the purpose is to summarise their relationship.

Confirm that paired values belong to the same observation unit. If a participant contributes multiple visits, a conventional correlation across all rows does not account for the dependence between those visits. A correlation between facility averages also answers a different question from a correlation between individual measurements.

State whether the objective concerns a linear relationship in the original values or an ordered relationship. The choice between Pearson and Spearman should reflect that objective and the observed form, not simply a threshold from a normality test.

13.3 Inspect the scatterplot first

Look for direction, form, spread, influential observations, and subgroups. The graphical-presentation chapter shows examples of a roughly linear relationship, a monotonic curve, and a U-shaped relationship. A single coefficient cannot communicate all those features.

A pooled association can depend on differences between subgroups rather than on the relationship within them. Inspect groups relevant to the question before treating a pooled coefficient as the whole explanation. This inspection does not itself adjust for confounding; it reveals a feature that may require a different analysis.

When a verified unusual observation strongly affects the coefficient, show or describe that influence. Do not remove it solely to obtain a preferred value. If a sensitivity analysis is justified, report the rule and its interpretation.

13.4 Pearson’s correlation coefficient

Pearson’s r describes linear association after scaling the covariance by the two standard deviations:

r=\frac{\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)} {\sqrt{\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2\sum_i(y_i-\bar y)^2}}.

It ranges from −1 to +1 when both variables have non-zero variation. A positive value indicates that larger values tend to occur together in a linear pattern; a negative value indicates the opposite direction. A constant variable does not provide the variation needed for this coefficient.

The coefficient is a descriptive calculation and does not require normality merely to exist. Its conventional small-sample test and interval use additional model assumptions. R documents the t-based Pearson test under independent observations from a suitable normal model and a Fisher-transformation interval. [30]

13.4.1 Direction and magnitude

The sign communicates direction. The absolute magnitude indicates how closely the observed values follow a linear relationship in their standardised scale. It does not indicate the slope in mmHg per year or the size of a clinical effect in original units.

Avoid using “weak,” “moderate,” or “strong” as universal clinical conclusions. Such labels are conventions, and their value depends on the measurement reliability, population range, and research objective. Report the numerical coefficient, uncertainty, sample size, and scatterplot context before adding any descriptive label.

13.4.2 Why zero does not mean no relationship

A near-zero Pearson coefficient can occur when positive and negative linear components balance in a curved pattern. In a symmetric U-shaped example, outcomes vary systematically with the predictor while the linear coefficient is zero. The graph is therefore necessary to distinguish lack of a linear association from lack of any relationship.

13.5 Spearman’s rank correlation

Spearman’s coefficient applies correlation to the ranks of the paired values. It describes monotonic association: whether values tend to move in one direction together, without requiring a straight-line pattern in the original units. Ties need an appropriate ranking and inferential calculation. [30]

An increasing curve can have a high rank correlation even when its original-scale relationship is not linear. A U-shaped relationship is not generally monotonic over its full range, so substituting Spearman for Pearson does not automatically summarise every curve well.

Choose Spearman because ranks and monotonic ordering match the question and data. Do not describe it as a method that ignores all assumptions or repairs invalid observation matching. Independence between pairs remains relevant to the conventional inferential calculation.

13.6 Inference for Pearson’s coefficient

For a conventional test of zero population correlation,

t=r\sqrt{\frac{n-2}{1-r^2}},\qquad df=n-2.

The p-value refers to that null model. It is not the probability that the correlation is causal or that a future sample will show the same magnitude.

An approximate interval can be formed using Fisher’s transformation:

z=\operatorname{atanh}(r),\qquad SE(z)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n-3}},

then transforming the interval limits back with the hyperbolic tangent. This is an interval for Pearson’s population correlation under the method’s assumptions. Do not use it as an unlabelled interval for a different coefficient.

The estimate indicates positive linear association, but the interval permits a range of magnitudes. It does not say that a one-year age increase changes blood pressure by 0.60 mmHg. That original-scale question requires a regression slope or another suitable model.

For comparison, an invented r=0.30 with the same 50 pairs has p = 0.0343 and a Fisher interval of approximately 0.024 to 0.534. A threshold label alone would hide the uncertainty in its magnitude. These are separate numerical teaching cases, not outcomes selected from one clinical dataset.

13.8 Correlation is not causation

An association may reflect a causal pathway, a common cause, selection, measurement structure, or other features of the study. A correlation coefficient does not distinguish these explanations. The design and substantive knowledge determine which interpretation is defensible.

For example, a relationship between age and blood pressure does not specify what would happen if one could intervene on age, nor does it isolate the influence of other characteristics. Later volumes address adjustment and causal reasoning. This volume reports the observed relationship with an explicit boundary on interpretation.

13.9 Correlation is not agreement

Two measurement methods can correlate perfectly while differing systematically. If method B equals method A plus 20 units for every observation, the linear correlation is +1, but the methods do not give the same values. A method-comparison question therefore requires assessment of differences and agreement, not correlation alone.

Reliability, agreement, and diagnostic accuracy have different targets. A high correlation does not establish that one method can replace another clinically. The relevant specialist methods are developed in later volumes.

13.10 Correlation matrices and missingness

A matrix can present correlations among several variables. Inspect which observations contribute to each pair and whether the sample sizes differ. A coefficient based on 50 complete pairs and another based on 35 pairs do not have the same information, even if they appear in one matrix.

jamovi’s correlation analysis provides Pearson, Spearman, and Kendall options, sample-size display, and graphical and uncertainty options. Verify which outputs apply to the selected coefficient and record the method. [54]

If many correlations are examined, distinguish the prespecified relationship from exploratory screening. Multiplicity and selective interpretation remain relevant; a matrix of flagged p-values is not a model-selection strategy by itself.

13.11 The relationship with simple regression

In an ordinary least-squares regression with one quantitative predictor, an intercept, and the same complete observations, R^2=r^2. This relationship does not make the two analyses interchangeable. Correlation is symmetric in the two variables, while regression specifies an outcome and produces a slope in defined units.

The regression chapter explains why those units, residuals, and prediction uncertainty matter. A numerical association summary should lead to a clearer question, not to an automatic causal explanation.

13.14 Review questions

  1. Can Pearson’s r be calculated for non-normal measurements?
  2. Does r close to zero rule out a U-shaped relationship?
  3. Why can perfect correlation coexist with poor agreement?
  4. What does r = 0.60 fail to tell the reader about the slope in original units?

13.15 Answers and reasoning

  1. Yes, if the required variation and data pairs exist. The conventional test and interval require additional assumptions.
  2. No. It summarises linear association, and opposite sides of the curve can balance.
  3. A systematic offset or scale difference can preserve linear association while changing the measured values.
  4. It does not provide the outcome change per predictor unit; that requires a model with the relevant units.
