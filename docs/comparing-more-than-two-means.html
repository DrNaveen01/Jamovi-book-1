11  Comparing More Than Two Groups

11.1 Learning objectives

After this chapter, the reader should be able to distinguish an overall comparison from a specific contrast, interpret a one-way ANOVA, recognise when unequal variances require a different method, and select compatible follow-up comparisons for mean-based or rank-based analyses.

11.2 Define groups, outcomes, and independence

Consider mean follow-up blood pressure across three independent groups. The outcome is continuous and the grouping variable has three levels. A one-way analysis considers one grouping factor. The number of groups does not change the need to define eligibility, units, missingness, and the observation structure.

Three measurements from the same participant are not three independent groups. Similarly, participants within shared facilities can have a clustered structure. These designs require an analysis that recognises their dependence, rather than an independent one-way ANOVA applied to every row.

Identify whether the objective is an overall assessment of group means or a particular comparison, such as intervention A versus a control. An overall test and a specific contrast answer different questions. Define the contrast when it is the primary scientific objective.

11.3 What a one-way ANOVA tests

The conventional one-way ANOVA evaluates the null hypothesis

H_0:\mu_1=\mu_2=\cdots=\mu_k.

Its alternative is that the means are not all equal. A small omnibus p-value does not mean that every pair differs. It also does not identify the size or direction of any particular contrast.

The fixed-effects one-way model expresses an observation as a group-specific mean component plus an error. The conventional model assumes independent errors with a common variance and a normal distribution for the relevant inference. [43]

11.4 Why the procedure is called analysis of variance

ANOVA compares variation between group means with variation within groups. If the observed groups differ substantially relative to the variation among observations within groups, the resulting F statistic becomes larger under the comparison with the null model.

The total squared variation is divided into between-group and within-group components. Each is converted to a mean square by dividing by its degrees of freedom. For k independent groups and N observations,

F=\frac{MS_{\text{between}}}{MS_{\text{within}}}, \qquad df_1=k-1,\quad df_2=N-k.

The F value is a test statistic, not a measurement of a clinical difference. Retain group summaries and the relevant contrast estimates when reporting the result.

11.5 Assess the conventional assumptions

Independence is established by design and data structure. A plot cannot prove it. Inspect whether participants contribute once to the comparison and whether matching or clustering needs to be accounted for.

Distributional assessment concerns within-group deviations or model residuals, rather than the pooled outcome distribution alone. Three groups with different means can create a non-normal-looking pooled distribution even when the within-group model is reasonable. Examine the appropriate residual or group plots.

The conventional model also assumes a common variance. Consider the observed spreads, sample sizes, and sensitivity to departures. A non-significant preliminary variance test does not prove that the variances are equal. Choosing a method solely from that preliminary p-value is an incomplete decision rule.

11.6 Welch’s one-way ANOVA

Welch’s one-way procedure compares group means while relaxing the common-variance assumption. It uses group-specific variability and an adjusted reference distribution. R’s oneway.test() documentation distinguishes the Welch approach from the equal-variance method, and jamovi’s simplified One-Way ANOVA offers a Welch option. [13, 44]

Welch’s method does not resolve dependence or a poor match between the intended estimand and the observed outcome. A skewed distribution with a few highly influential values still requires assessment. Report the selected method and its numerator and denominator degrees of freedom as displayed, including non-integer values where appropriate.

Choose follow-up procedures consistent with the variance model. A method based on a pooled common variance should not be added automatically after selecting an unequal-variance analysis.

11.7 Calculated three-group illustration

The following summaries are invented for teaching:

Group n Mean, mmHg SD, mmHg
A 20 125.0 10.0
B 20 130.0 10.0
C 20 135.0 10.0

The overall mean is 130.0 mmHg. The between-group sum of squares is 1,000; the within-group sum of squares is 5,700. Dividing by 2 and 57 degrees of freedom gives mean squares of 500 and 100. The conventional F statistic is therefore 5.00, with df = 2 and 57, and p = 0.00998.

The analysis indicates incompatibility with the equal-means model under its assumptions. It does not establish that A differs from B, B differs from C, and A differs from C individually. Those contrasts require their own estimates and inferential procedure.

The sample eta-squared summary is

\eta^2=\frac{SS_{\text{between}}}{SS_{\text{total}}} =\frac{1000}{6700}=0.149.

This describes the between-group share of the observed squared variation in this example. It is not the percentage of participants who improved or a causal percentage explained by treatment. Prefer original-scale contrast estimates when interpreting the health question.

11.8 Planned contrasts and post-hoc comparisons

A planned contrast is specified from the research objective before inspecting the results. For example, a study may compare the average of two intervention groups with a control. Its coefficients and uncertainty calculation should match that defined comparison.

Post-hoc pairwise comparisons assess particular pairs, often after an overall exploration. With three groups there are three distinct pairs; with four there are six. State which family of comparisons is being evaluated and which adjustment or simultaneous interval procedure is used.

Tukey’s procedure provides simultaneous intervals for pairwise mean differences under its model. Games–Howell is an unequal-variance pairwise approach available in jamovi’s One-Way ANOVA documentation. These methods serve different assumptions and should be selected consistently with the main analysis. [13, 45]

A scientifically planned contrast need not be treated as a question that exists only after a significant omnibus test. Explain the inferential plan and multiplicity control appropriate to that objective. Conversely, reporting only the favourable pair after many unreported comparisons is selective reporting.

11.9 Multiple comparisons and error control

An adjustment applies to a defined family of analyses. Bonferroni controls a familywise error rate by a simple correction; Holm is a sequential familywise procedure. False-discovery-rate methods address a different error criterion. The intended criterion and family should be stated. [37]

Do not describe an adjusted p-value without identifying what was adjusted. A correction across the three group pairs is different from one across several outcomes, subgroup comparisons, and time points. The analysis plan should define the relevant family rather than allow it to change to obtain a preferred result.

An adjustment also does not turn an exploratory analysis into a prespecified confirmatory one. Preserve that distinction in the interpretation.

11.10 Kruskal–Wallis test

The Kruskal–Wallis procedure uses ranks for comparisons across independent groups. It is appropriate for a defined rank-based or distributional question under its assumptions. Its location or median interpretation requires additional conditions about distributional shapes; it should not automatically be described as an ANOVA of medians. [46]

As with ANOVA, a global result does not identify which groups differ. Follow-up rank comparisons require a compatible method and a declared multiplicity approach. R documents pairwise Wilcoxon comparisons with p-value adjustment as one implementation route; the procedure chosen in jamovi should be identified by its actual name and version. [47, 48]

Inspect group distributions before using a pure location interpretation. If one group differs mainly in spread and another in location, a global rank result may reflect more than a difference in central values. Show medians and quartile limits as descriptions without claiming that these alone define the test’s null hypothesis.

11.11 Reading jamovi output

Confirm the continuous outcome and grouping variable, then identify the conventional, Welch, or non-parametric procedure actually requested. Read group counts, summaries, diagnostic information, the overall statistic, and relevant contrast output. Check how missing observations were excluded. [13, 48]

When pairwise tables are present, verify the reference order of each difference, the CI or adjustment method, and the family to which the comparisons belong. Different contrast directions can coexist in one output table; a sign should never be interpreted without its labels.

11.12 Reporting example

For the calculated conventional illustration: “The invented mean follow-up values were 125.0, 130.0, and 135.0 mmHg in groups A, B, and C, respectively (20 observations per group; SD 10.0 in each group). A conventional one-way ANOVA gave F(2, 57) = 5.00, p = 0.010. The overall comparison does not identify the individual group contrasts; these would require the prespecified follow-up analysis.”

For a real report, replace the final teaching statement with the relevant contrast estimates and intervals. State why the selected variance model and observation structure are appropriate, and identify the follow-up method if one is used.

11.13 Common errors

Avoid applying independent ANOVA to repeated measurements, describing the omnibus result as evidence that all pairs differ, choosing post-hoc methods that contradict the variance model, treating Kruskal–Wallis as an unconditional median test, or reporting pairwise p-values without the adjustment strategy.

11.15 Review questions

  1. What does the alternative hypothesis of a one-way ANOVA mean?
  2. Why are three follow-up measurements from the same participant not three independent groups?
  3. What must be identified when reporting an adjusted pairwise p-value?
  4. Does a Kruskal–Wallis result always establish different medians?

11.16 Answers and reasoning

  1. The means are not all equal; it does not assert that every pair is different.
  2. Measurements are linked within participants and require an analysis that retains that dependence.
  3. State the procedure, the family of comparisons, and the direction of the contrast.
  4. No. The method uses ranks, and a median-shift interpretation requires additional distributional conditions.
