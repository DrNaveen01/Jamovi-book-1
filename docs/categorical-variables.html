12  Associations Between Categorical Variables

12.1 Learning objectives

After this chapter, the reader should be able to construct a contingency table, choose the correct denominator, distinguish independent from paired categorical observations, assess expected counts, and interpret a difference or ratio on the scale supported by the study design.

12.2 Start with the design and denominator

Consider whether an event occurs more often in group A than in group B. For an independent comparison, each participant contributes one group assignment and one event status. The table describes two categorical variables observed on the same set of independent participants.

Before calculating a percentage, establish who was eligible to contribute to the event denominator and whether status is known. A recorded absence is different from an unknown outcome. If follow-up is incomplete, a simple event proportion may fail to answer the intended risk question.

The study design determines the interpretation. An event proportion over defined follow-up can describe risk when its denominator and ascertainment support that meaning. A cross-sectional proportion may describe prevalence. A rate uses an appropriate person-time denominator and is a different quantity. In a case-control sample, selection by outcome means the sampled case proportion is not the population risk.

12.3 Construct and label the contingency table

The following independent-group example is invented for teaching. Assume complete event status over the same defined follow-up in both groups:

Group Event present Event absent Total
A 30 70 100
B 15 85 100
Total 45 155 200

The group-specific event proportions are 30/100 = 30% and 15/100 = 15%. These are row percentages in this orientation. The fraction of all event cases belonging to A is 30/45 = 66.7%, a different denominator and question.

State the reference direction. The calculations below compare A with B. Reversing that direction changes the sign of a difference and takes the reciprocal of a ratio.

12.4 Pearson chi-square test of independence

The null model states that the two categorical variables are independent. It specifies expected cell counts calculated from the row and column totals:

E_{ij}=\frac{(\text{row total}_i)(\text{column total}_j)}{N}.

The test statistic summarises the observed deviations from these expected counts:

\chi^2=\sum_{i,j}\frac{(O_{ij}-E_{ij})^2}{E_{ij}}, \qquad df=(r-1)(c-1).

This is a test of association, not a measure of the magnitude of the association. R’s documentation describes the Pearson procedure, continuity correction for a 2-by-2 table, and a simulation-based option. [49]

In the invented table, the expected event count in each group is 22.5, and the expected absent count is 77.5. The uncorrected Pearson statistic is 6.452 with one degree of freedom, giving p = 0.0111. The estimate of association still needs to be reported separately.

12.5 Expected counts and sparse tables

The adequacy of the chi-square approximation concerns expected counts, not simply observed counts or overall sample size. A large total sample can still contain sparse cells when one category is rare. Examine the table dimensions and the distribution of expected counts.

One commonly used screening convention checks for no expected count below one and a high proportion of expected counts at least five. R’s documented hybrid Fisher procedure uses more than 80% at least five and none below one. These are approximation rules within a defined procedure, not a universal guarantee of validity for every table. [50]

For a sparse 2-by-2 table, Fisher’s exact procedure is a common option. For larger tables, an appropriate exact or Monte Carlo procedure may be needed, subject to computational feasibility and software availability. State the method and, for simulations, the number of repetitions and relevant reproducibility settings.

Yates continuity correction applies to a 2-by-2 chi-square calculation. It is not a general repair for sparse tables of arbitrary dimensions. Select the procedure using observation structure, dimensions, expected counts, and the question rather than overall sample size alone. [49]

Do not combine categories merely to make a test run unless the grouping is substantively defensible. Category merging changes the question and should be documented.

12.6 Fisher’s exact procedure

Fisher’s test evaluates the table conditional on its margins under the independence model. For the invented 2-by-2 table, the two-sided p-value is 0.0171. This differs from the uncorrected Pearson p-value because the procedures use different inferential calculations.

An “exact” calculation does not remove the need for appropriate observation structure or eliminate every uncertainty about design. The term identifies how the probability is calculated under the model. The two-sided definition and reported odds-ratio estimator can also vary by procedure; R’s Fisher documentation uses a conditional estimate rather than the simple cross-product ratio. [50]

When reporting, identify the chosen procedure rather than selecting whichever p-value is smaller. The point estimate and interval should also be labelled with their calculation method.

12.7 Risk or prevalence difference

Under the stated teaching follow-up, the risk difference is

RD=\frac{30}{100}-\frac{15}{100}=0.15.

This is 15 percentage points, not a 15% relative increase. Its large-sample Wald 95% CI in this illustration is approximately 3.6 to 26.4 percentage points. The absolute difference communicates how much the group-specific proportions differ on the original probability scale.

If the same table arose from a cross-sectional study, use an interpretation appropriate to prevalence rather than follow-up risk. If it arose from outcome-based sampling, do not calculate a population risk difference from the sampled case fractions.

12.8 Risk ratio and odds ratio

For the teaching follow-up table,

RR=\frac{30/100}{15/100}=2.00.

The group A event risk is twice that in B under the stipulated design and complete follow-up. A log-scale large-sample 95% CI is 1.15 to 3.48.

Odds are probability divided by its complement. Group A odds are 30/70; group B odds are 15/85. The sample odds ratio is

OR=\frac{30/70}{15/85}=\frac{30\times85}{70\times15}=2.43.

Its log-Wald 95% CI is 1.21 to 4.87. The odds ratio and risk ratio differ because odds and probability differ. An OR of 2.43 here is not a statement that the event probability is 2.43 times as high. Bland and Altman’s Statistics Note explains the odds-ratio calculation and interpretation. [51]

The approximation of an OR to an RR depends on the outcome probabilities and context; it should not be assumed without checking. Always name the reported measure and reference category.

12.8.1 Interpret interval methods carefully

The Wald intervals above are teaching calculations appropriate to the stated positive cell counts and large-sample approximation. They are not presented as the exact intervals returned by Fisher’s procedure. With small or zero counts, the simple log formulas can fail or perform poorly, requiring a suitable alternative method.

Measure in the example Estimate Interpretation of A relative to B
Risk difference 0.15 15 percentage points higher risk
Risk ratio 2.00 Twice the risk
Odds ratio 2.43 2.43 times the odds

The numerical direction is the same, but the magnitudes are not interchangeable. A reporting table should not use one generic heading such as “relative risk” for all three quantities.

12.9 Larger contingency tables

For more than two rows or columns, the overall chi-square test evaluates independence across the complete table. Its result does not identify a particular category as the cause of the association. Examine observed counts, relevant percentages, and prespecified comparisons before drawing a category-specific conclusion.

Cramér’s V can summarise the strength of an association without giving its clinical direction. It is not a risk ratio, an odds ratio, or the proportion of participants with an event. Prefer question-specific contrasts when available and substantively useful.

jamovi’s contingency-table analysis can display observed and expected counts, percentages, test results, and several association measures. Select the quantities appropriate to the table and design; an available checkbox does not establish that every measure is meaningful for that study. [22]

12.10 Paired binary outcomes: McNemar’s question

If the same participants have a binary status before and after an observation period, the data are paired. A 2-by-2 table now cross-classifies before and after status rather than two independent groups. McNemar’s procedure evaluates marginal symmetry and uses the discordant pairs for the comparison. [52]

In an invented table, suppose 20 participants have the event at both times, 15 change from present to absent, 5 change from absent to present, and 60 are absent at both times. There are 100 pairs, but only the 20 discordant pairs inform the change-direction comparison. The before proportion is 35% and the after proportion is 25%.

An independent Pearson test applied as if the before and after samples were unrelated would discard the pairing. Use a paired categorical procedure, with an exact conditional approach considered when the discordant counts require it. A binomial calculation on the discordant counts is one exact route under the paired null model. [26, 53]

12.11 Reporting example

For the independent teaching illustration: “The event occurred in 30 of 100 participants in group A (30.0%) and 15 of 100 in B (15.0%). Under the stipulated common follow-up, the risk ratio for A versus B was 2.00 (log-Wald 95% CI 1.15–3.48), and the absolute risk difference was 15.0 percentage points (Wald 95% CI 3.6–26.4). The uncorrected Pearson chi-square test gave χ²(1) = 6.45, p = 0.011.”

This statement names the denominators, measures, directions, interval methods, and test. It does not describe the association as causal. If a different design or procedure is used, revise those elements rather than transplanting this wording unchanged.

12.13 Review questions

  1. Which counts determine whether the chi-square approximation may be inadequate?
  2. Does a large total sample guarantee that all table cells have adequate expected counts?
  3. In the invented table, why are RR = 2.00 and OR = 2.43 different?
  4. Which observations inform McNemar’s comparison in the paired example?

12.14 Answers and reasoning

  1. Expected counts under the independence model, assessed with table dimensions and the distribution of sparse cells.
  2. No. A rare category can create sparse expected counts despite a large total.
  3. The RR compares probabilities; the OR compares probability divided by its complement.
  4. The 15 present-to-absent and 5 absent-to-present pairs. The concordant pairs do not contribute to that direction comparison.
